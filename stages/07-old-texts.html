Really old texts

Read really old Japanese texts. They are hard, and they stay hard. That is exactly why modern Japanese feels easy afterwards.

Why it works

  • Depth

    Old texts show where modern words and kanji came from. You see a word in its first form, and the modern form starts to make sense.

  • Stamina

    Old grammar and old spelling slow you down a lot. When you can crawl through them, a modern newspaper feels easy.

  • Culture in the original

    Many references in modern Japanese point back to these texts. Poems, set phrases, and jokes quote them. Now you get the references.

What it feels like

It feels like stage 2 again, but harder. The grammar differs from modern Japanese, and old texts use some kana in old ways.

Some days you read only three lines. Those three lines still count.

Signs it is working

  • You recognize old grammar forms when they show up in modern writing.
  • You read a short old passage and get the general meaning.
  • Modern Japanese prose feels smooth and fast.
  • You start to have favorite old authors.

Cute mistakes to skip

  • Do not start with the hardest text. Pick a short one that interests you.
  • Do not give up after a bad week. Crawl if you must.
  • Do not read only modern translations. Read the original next to them.

Why 古文 and 漢文 at all

Two old languages hide inside modern Japanese. 古文 is old Japanese: the grammar of the 平安 court and the medieval diaries. 漢文 is classical Chinese read in Japanese word order. Modern Japanese is built on top of both, and it never removed them.

  • 古文 is still spoken

    べし, なり, ず, and けり live on in set phrases, in song lyrics, in legal text, and in jokes. When you know the old forms, formal modern Japanese stops being a list of exceptions.

  • 漢文 built the vocabulary

    Most two-kanji words follow classical Chinese word order, not Japanese. 読書 is read-book, verb then object, which is Chinese. Once you read 漢文, you can take a new word apart and guess what it means.

  • Together, they are the ceiling

    Nothing in modern Japanese is harder than this. When you have read an old text, there is no register left that can scare you. That is the real reason this stage exists.

Where to find old texts

True 古文

  • 方丈記 on Aozora Bunko: a hermit's hut and a burning city, in about ten pages. The classic first 古文 text. Aozora has the original, and a modern translation as a separate card.
  • 枕草子: lists of things that are charming, things that are annoying, and the first line that every Japanese child knows. Read one list a day.

Old, in a different way: the 巫研 wiki

docs.miko.org collects out-of-copyright texts on folklore, shrines, and literature. Its authors, such as 折口信夫 and 中山太郎, wrote in the 明治 and 大正 eras. That is not 古文, but it is old prose, with old kanji, old kana spelling, and sentences that run for half a page. It sits between the modern novel and the true classics, and the topics are strange and wonderful. Dive in.

  • Start with 日本巫女史, a history of shrine maidens. Read a chapter that sounds odd. Then read why it is not odd.
  • Browse the author categories. Each author is a different kind of difficult.

The order that works

明治 prose from the wiki first. Then 方丈記. Then 枕草子. Then whatever you fall in love with. Keep the 漢文 stories from stage 5 going beside all of it.

The step: Read really old texts, at any speed. Crawl if you must, but do not give up.

  • You read one whole short classical work in the original, with a modern text beside it, and you finished it.
  • You can read a 書き下し文 of a 漢文 passage and follow it without the translation.
  • A modern newspaper feels like a picture book.
