A primer on old text

般若心経 and 魏志倭人伝

Carry two short classical texts beside the whole road. Read them slowly, and form your own interpretation of each one.

Why it works

  • Short, but deep

    般若心経 is a Buddhist sutra of about 260 characters. 魏志倭人伝 is a passage of about 2,000 characters in a Chinese history book. You can hold each one in your head, and you can return to it for years.

  • Classical Chinese, early

    Both texts are classical Chinese. Japanese grew up beside this language, so it explains many kanji, idioms, and old words. Stage 4 gives you a small taste of it. These texts go deeper.

  • Your own reading stays

    When you build your own interpretation, you process every character actively. A borrowed explanation fades. An explanation that you built yourself stays with you.

What it feels like

般若心経 feels strange. People in Japan chant it straight through, with the Chinese-style reading of each character. The meaning hides behind the sound.

魏志倭人伝 feels like a puzzle. It describes ancient Japan from the outside, and it names 卑弥呼 and the land that scholars now call 邪馬台国. Scholars still argue about parts of it, so you may keep your own reading.

Crawl if you must. 魏志倭人伝 waited about 1,700 years for you. It can wait a bit longer~ (๑・∀・๑)

Signs it is working

  • You recognize characters from these texts in other places.
  • You can say what each text means to you, in a few sentences.
  • Your interpretation changes as your Japanese grows.
  • You catch yourself reading one of them again, just for fun.

Your own take on a sutra. How very grown-up of you~

Cute mistakes to skip

  • Do not copy an interpretation from the internet and call it yours.
  • Do not try to finish these texts in one week. They run beside stages 2 to 7.
  • Do not give up because the first reading makes no sense. The first reading never does.

More 漢文, the easy kind

Do not stop at these two texts. Japanese schools teach classical Chinese through short stories, and many of those stories became four-character idioms in stage 4. Each one is a few lines long. Each one has a punchline. Read them beside the sutra, as a snack.

  • 矛盾 a spear that pierces anything meets a shield that nothing pierces. This is where the word for contradiction comes from.
  • 守株 a farmer waits by a tree stump for a second rabbit. On luck, and on people who wait for it twice.
  • 五十歩百歩 a soldier who ran fifty steps laughs at one who ran a hundred. From Mencius. You already use it in stage 4.
  • 推敲 a poet cannot decide between push and knock. The origin of the Japanese word for polishing a text.
  • 蛇足 a man loses a drinking contest because he paints feet on his snake. On doing too much.
  • 杞憂 a man in Qi cannot sleep because the sky might fall. Needless worry, in two characters.
  • 論語 the Analects. Pick the first chapter. 学而時習之: learn, and practice it in time. Five characters, and they already describe this road.

Read each one three times. First the original characters. Then the 書き下し文, the Japanese word-order rewrite that schools use. Then the original again. On the third pass the characters start to speak in order.

Six stories about people making fools of themselves, and one sutra. Balanced diet~ ( ¬‿¬)

The step: Along the way, read 般若心経 and 魏志倭人伝. Form your own interpretation of each one.

  • You can read 般若心経 aloud from start to end and say, in your own words, what it means to you.
  • You can tell the story of 魏志倭人伝: who the 倭 were, what they ate, and who 卑弥呼 was.
  • You have read at least three of the short 漢文 stories below, and one of them made you smile.
